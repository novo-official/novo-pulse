The problem
You are looking at 32% of the demand and deciding as if it were all of it.
Today Check-in night
Travel demand keeps forming until the night itself. At any decision point only part of it has been searched — the rest has not happened yet.

Travel demand does not appear at once — it keeps building right up to the check-in night. So on any given day, a destination team looking at their dashboard sees only the searches logged so far. In our data that is 32%. The other 68% has not happened yet.

That creates two failures. You spot a rising destination too late. And when you do see a forecast, you cannot tell how much of it is recorded fact and how much is a model's guess.

Transition: So we did not build a model that re-predicts total demand. We built one that separates what is known from what is not.

The solution

Separate the fact from the forecast

Final Demand = Observed So Far + Forecast Remaining

Orange is what has genuinely been searched — the model never touches it. Blue is what has not happened yet; that is the only thing we forecast. The dark line is the sum, and that is our output.

Two consequences. First, the number can never contradict reality — it is structurally impossible for our forecast to fall below searches that actually happened. Second, the team always sees how much of a number is fact and how much is estimate. For 321 destinations across the next 30 nights: 32.2% observed, 67.8% still to come.

Do not say the blue band is uncertainty or a confidence interval. It is a point forecast of remaining demand.

What makes this different

Three times we chose the honest number over the better-looking one

We kept our worst week in the score Removing the war-overlap window would have shown 10.61% instead of 14.79%. We report the number that includes the regime we cannot predict.
We built a better model and threw it away Clustering made the score look 2% better. We checked: 89.8% of that gain was an accounting artefact, not real accuracy. Real gain was 0.21% — below our pre-declared bar, so it did not ship.
The forecast tells you when not to trust it One day out, error is 2.2%. Thirty days out, 37.5%. So the product ships a usage rule, not one number: D1–7 act · D8–21 monitor · D22–30 prioritise only.

Anyone can show you a good number. What should make you trust a team is what they do when the number could be made to look better.

One. We kept the war week. It costs us four points of headline accuracy.

Two. We built a clustering model that pooled low-data destinations, and the score improved. Then we checked whether the improvement was real — when you merge rows, positive and negative errors cancel out and the metric flatters you for free. 89.8% of the gain was exactly that. The genuine modelling gain was 0.21%, below the 2% bar we set before running it. So we dropped it, and kept city-level detail the business actually needs.

Three. Most forecasts hand you one number with no instructions. Ours states where it is reliable and where it is only a ranking signal.

Why we dropped the more sophisticated model

89.8%of the apparent gain was mechanical, not real
0.21%genuine modelling gain — our bar was 2%

Clustering pools low-data destinations so each group has more signal. The score improved from 12.0168% to 11.7699%. But merging rows lets positive and negative errors cancel before the absolute value is taken — the metric improves even with an unchanged model. We measured that by taking the city model's own predictions and simply summing them onto the same groups: that alone captured 89.8% of the gain. Real modelling gain: 0.21%, against a 2% bar declared in advance.

Caveat: those figures are a three-fold uncalibrated sweep, comparable only inside that experiment. If pressed — on five identical folds with identical calibration: city 0.1479 vs cluster 0.1487. Clustering is still not better.
